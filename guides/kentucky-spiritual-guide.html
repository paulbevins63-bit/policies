ChurchesAbove the beamThe Churches screen — all churches near you (Part 6).

The strip along the bottom

Beneath the cross, before you’ve entered any emergency information, is ＋ Set Up Emergency Info; tap it to fill in your contacts and medical details. Once anything is filled in, it is replaced by a small red Emergency Information badge in the bottom-left corner. Your contacts’ names and numbers never show on the home screen, where anyone holding the phone could read them: they are one tap away, on the Emergency Information screen, which also has a button to share them. On a recovery milestone day, a small gold line may also appear; that’s a date calculation, never anything you have to set up. About every 90 days a small card may appear above it, Updates may be ready, with Check and Not now. It is a date calculation too: showing it contacts no one (Part 11).

Where Recovery lives, and what sits where it used to

The Recovery button is on the Home screen. It used to appear a second time inside Personal Assistance; that slot now holds Dire Need, a shelf of short prayers for the worst of it. Dire Need is the one button on that screen that works with no location set — a prayer doesn’t need to know what town you’re in.

Part 2

Prayers — and how to find them

Tap Prayers on the home screen to open the Prayers hub. Across the top sit a search box — “Type a word, or what you want to pray for…” — and a Faiths button. Under them, Praying offers three choices: Alone, Together and All (“Every prayer, for one person or for a group”), and then nine doors: The Daily Walk, My Personal Prayers, Nightly Reflection, People, When It’s Hard, Recovery, Hurting, Before God and The Church’s Words. Eight fit the screen at once; the ninth is a short scroll down.

There are more than 2,300 prayers here, in over a hundred collections, and 179 of them were written right here in Kentucky. The collection grows with app updates. Every door opens on shelves that carry their own count, so you know what is behind one before you tap it — under Hurting, for instance, Dire Need (23) comes first on purpose, then Fear & Worry (70), Comfort (98), Surrender (223), and on down through When God Feels Far (32) and The Circling Mind (30).

Some prayers come in two voices. Those open with two tabs at the top — Modern 2026 (plain, present-day wording) and Original Historic (the traditional text) — and you move between them by tapping a tab or swiping. The credit line under the prayer changes with the tab, and on some records so does the title: Help My Unbelief becomes Help Thou Mine Unbelief on the historic side.

Keeping your own

My Personal Prayers is your own shelf. Along its top are Curated Guidance, Dire Need — gold, in the middle, for the night you can’t aim your thumb — and Bootleggers Only. Tap the star at the bottom of any prayer to keep it here, and use + Add Prayer to write your own or bring one in from a .txt file or Google Drive. Press and hold one of your own to remove it.

“How do I search for a prayer?”

Type it. The search box at the top of the Prayers hub takes a word or a whole phrase — what you want to pray for, in your own words — and searches the whole collection. You can also narrow by tradition with Faiths, which lists each one with its count: Protestant, Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Recovery, Anglican, Lutheran, Methodist, Assyrian, Armenian Apostolic, Oriental Orthodox, Russian Orthodox, Baptist, African Methodist Episcopal, and All. Browsing still works, and for many people it works better: the nine doors and their shelves are arranged by what a day feels like rather than by title.

Part 3

The Bible & installing offline Bibles

Tap Bibles to open the Bible screen. At the top is a search box (Search keywords… with a gold GO), plus Bookmarks and History buttons. Two kinds of Bibles live here:

Online Bibles (internet required)

A panel of well-known translations — NIV, ESV, NLT, RSV, NABRE, NJB, NAB and more — that open on BibleGateway.com. These need a connection; the app links out to them so the publishers’ copyrights stay with the publishers. Tapping Other Bibles reveals five more online versions: NKJV, NASB, NRSV, CSB, and the Jerusalem Bible (JB).

Offline Bibles (no internet, once installed)

Four complete Bibles are already installed and work with no connection at all: King James Version, Berean Literal Bible, Catholic Public Domain Version, and Douay-Rheims (Complete).

Many more can be added for free. Tap Other Bibles, and you’ll find three sections:

  • More Offline Bibles — 10 complete Bibles (full Old and New Testament): ASV with Strong’s, Berean Standard, Bishops’, Coverdale, Darby, Bible in Basic English, World English Bible, Geneva, KJV with Strong’s, and the Literal Standard Version (LSV).
  • Partial Offline Bibles — Old-Testament-only (Brenton Septuagint, JPS Tanakh 1917), New-Testament-only (Revised Young’s Literal NT), and incomplete works (Open English Bible, Tyndale Bible). Each one explains exactly which books are included right under its button.
  • More Online Bibles — the five extra BibleGateway versions noted above.
How to install an offline Bible

Tap any Bible that isn’t installed yet. A window titled Not Installed appears with a short note about what’s included and asks: “Install {name} now? It’s free and works fully offline once installed.” Tap Yes, Install. A brief spinner shows while it loads onto your phone — no internet is used, the text is already bundled in the app. When it’s done, the Bible gets a ✓ and moves to the top of the list; tap it to start reading. To undo, tap Not Now instead.

An Aa button opens a Text Size slider (12–32 pt) so you can make the words as large as you need.

Jumping to a chapter

While you read, the book title at the top is a button — it says “tap for chapters” right beneath it. Tap it and a list of chapters opens — one row per chapter, reading “Chapter 1”, “Chapter 2” and on down, with a drag bar down the side for the long books. Tap a row to jump straight there. Psalm 140 is one tap away, not a hundred swipes. The ‹ and › arrows beside the title step one chapter at a time.

Part 4

Searching Bible passages

The search box lives at the top of the Bibles screen. Type a word or phrase into Search keywords… and tap GO.

  • The Search Results screen shows each match as “Book chapter:verse” in gold with the verse text beneath. Tap any result to jump straight to that verse in the reader.
  • Search runs against the offline King James Version index on your phone — so it works with no internet. Any offline Bible you’ve installed can be read fully offline as well.
  • When a reading names a verse like “James 1:5,” its Relate to Bible button pulls that verse straight from the offline KJV, without a search (Part 5).

Remember: this keyword search is for the Bible. Prayers are browsed by title (Part 2).

Part 5

Recovery Readings & Church Readings

Recovery Readings

These are daily recovery meditations. You can reach them two ways:

  • The Recovery Readings button on the home screen (left arm of the cross), and
  • Inside Recovery, via the Daily Readings button.

Both open the same list. It gathers well-known daily readings — AA Daily Reflections, NA Just For Today and Spiritual Principle a Day, several Al-Anon readings, Hazelden meditations (Twenty-Four Hours a Day, Touchstones, Today’s Gift, and more), plus a small “Other Daily Readings” section (Daily Zen, Buddhist Daily Wisdom). Each opens in the app’s reader, with the two Relate buttons along the bottom (below).

Church Readings

Tap Church Readings (right arm of the cross) for daily lectionary and devotional readings, grouped under headers by tradition: Anglican, Baptist, Catholic, Christian in General, Episcopalian, Lutheran, Presbyterian, and Orthodox. The Catholic section is the largest — Mass readings from the USCCB, homilies, Sacred Space, several devotionals, and a Franciscan Media set. One Catholic item, Order of the Mass, opens an outline of the Mass: the parts in order, so you can see the shape of it. Each gold line opens that part of the Mass on Universalis, a licensed publisher of the Mass text. Reading 1, the Responsorial Psalm, Reading 2 and the Gospel open the day’s readings on the USCCB’s website. Reading 2 is read on Sundays and holy days; on other days that line goes to the Gospel and says there is no second reading today. Once a page is open, the round ☰ button at its top right jumps to any major part of the Mass without going back, and on the Order of Mass the arrows above and below it step to the previous or next place the people speak, so you can keep up with your responses. The Mass text is not carried in the app, so this one needs a connection.

Relating a reading to you

A reading open in the app’s reader shows a bar along the bottom with Relate to Reading and Relate to Bible. Both stay greyed until the page has finished loading. Either one opens a sheet, Relate this reading, with two buttons, and the answer appears straight away, put together on your phone from the page itself:

  • Relate this to me shows “From the reading:” and up to three sentences quoted exactly from the reading, then two questions to sit with: “How do these words connect with what you are facing today?” and “What is one small step you would like to take?”
  • Relate this to the Bible shows a King James verse with its reference (the verse the reading names, or one chosen to go with it), then two questions: “What connection do you see between this Scripture and the reading?” and “Considering what you are facing today, what might you put into practice?” If no verse can be found, it shows the reading’s own words instead.

Save to Journal under an answer keeps it in your Notebook. Nothing in the answer is made up: the words are the reading’s own, or the Bible’s.

On the home screen and inside Recovery

Recovery Readings appear in both places on purpose — from the home cross and again inside the Recovery menu — so a daily reading is never more than a tap or two away.

Part 6

Churches & the four location buttons

The Churches screen finds places of worship near you. Along the top is a location dock with four buttons. Here’s exactly what each does.

ButtonWhat happens when you tap it
My Location Uses GPS to find where you are. The first time, it shows a short notice (“Use your location?”) explaining that your location is sent to Google, to look up your town, and to OpenStreetMap, to search for nearby places — tap Allow to continue, or Not now to back out. You’ll see status like “Connecting to GPS…” then “Location: {city}, {state}.” (More on this in Part 10.)
Zip Code Opens Enter 5-Digit Zip Code. Type a ZIP (e.g. 42701) and tap Search — no GPS needed. Works even offline, using the ZIP database built into the app.
Select State Opens a Select Region list (the United States and the U.S. Territories) so you can browse an area by hand, with no location access at all. In Churches, pick a state and then one of its counties: the list comes back showing the churches around that county.
All USA Search Opens the Resource Search screen — free 211 help and a national services directory (covered in Part 7).

Results start within about 20 miles and are sorted nearest-first. A Expand Search (50 mi) button widens the radius. Church data comes from two places: IRS-registered non-profit records bundled by state, and the OpenStreetMap community map. A Faith: filter lets you narrow to a tradition (All Faiths, Orthodox, Baptist, Catholic, Methodist, and so on). Cards show the church name, a faith label, a green Offers Assistance badge when a congregation helps the needy, a tappable address that opens maps, and website links. You can tap Not there to flag a listing that’s wrong.

Call before you go

The app itself reminds you: “Call before you go — service times can change.” Some listings come from community maps and may be outdated; rows that look questionable are marked “verify before going.” Always confirm before making a trip.

“Churches” is not “Church Aid”

The home-screen Churches button lists all churches near you. The Church Aid button (inside Personal Assistance, Part 7) is different — it lists only churches that offer help like food, clothing, shelter, or jobs. Same neighborhood, different purpose.

It has its own location dock with the same four buttons as Churches — My Location, Zip Code, Select State, All USA Search — and they work exactly the same way (Part 6). A short line explains: “Find services automatically or browse by area.” Below the dock is a row of chips for any locations you’ve saved before, so you can switch back instantly.

Adding a resource yourself

Add Resources lets you save a free or low-cost resource so others can find it — it stays on your phone. Four ways in:

  • Scan a Page — photograph a flyer or listing; the app reads the text on-device and pulls out the details.
  • Upload a File — read a file already on your phone.
  • Browse a Website — paste a web address and the app pulls out the name, phone, and address.
  • Manually Enter or Edit Data — type it in or fix it yourself, choosing a category.

All USA Search (the 211 screen)

All USA Search opens Resource Search. At the top is 211 — free help: Call 211 (Free) (dials the free 24/7 helpline), Text 211 (Free), and Kentucky 211 Online. Type a term and you also get Search FindHelp (Free) — a national directory of free/reduced-cost services. Below that, On-Device Results searches the resources saved on your phone. Nothing on this screen costs anything, and nothing here asks you to sign up for an account or a key.

Verify before you go — especially crisis resources

The app is blunt about this: “Verify all resources before you go. Listings may be outdated.” For crisis categories it goes further — “⚠ CALL TO VERIFY BEFORE YOU GO. Crisis resources change fast. Make sure your shelter is safe, open, and right for you. DO NOT GO without confirming.” Please take that seriously.

  • Recovery Contacts — your call-first list. As the screen puts it: “When you’re tempted to use — or to do something worse — reach out to someone here BEFORE you act.” Add sponsors, your home group, crisis lines, sober friends; tap a number to call.
Part 9

Your Notebook & personal information

Tap Notebook to open My Journal. The first time, a short “Welcome to Your Journal” form asks for the basics — a preferred name, your location, and a recovery phone / main contact. After that you get the journal composer: an optional title, a big “What’s on your mind today?” box, and a Submit Entry button. Recent entries appear as chips you can reopen.

What the Notebook is for

Two things: a place to write and keep private journal entries, and a personal profile of the things about you that you choose to keep here. Tap Personal Info (top-right) to open the full profile.

What the profile holds

Right at the top, the app makes a promise:

The app’s own promise

Everything here is optional and stays on your phone.

Every field is optional. The profile includes: Basics (what to call you, location, phone); Milestones (clean date, birthday — dates only); About Me (family, pets, hobbies, favorite books / music / movies, meaningful places); and Faith (your background, in your own words). None of it is required, and what you fill in is kept to serve you, on your device.

There’s also an Emergency Info / Medical ID editor (reachable from the home screen’s ＋ Set Up Emergency Info, or its red Emergency Information badge once anything is filled in). It holds emergency contacts and optional medical details (blood type, allergies, medications, conditions, physician). It promises: “This stays on your phone. Nothing from it shows on the front page — when anything here is filled in, a small red Emergency Information button appears in the corner, and that opens this screen.” A Share button builds a plain-text summary and opens Android’s share sheet — sharing only ever happens when you tap it.

Part 10

“Exact Location” — how it’s used, and why

When you tap My Location, the app uses your phone’s GPS to get a precise position. Here’s the whole story, plainly.

  • What it’s used for: (1) turning your position into a city and state to show you, which Google looks up; (2) sorting and filtering nearby churches and resources by distance on your phone; and (3) asking OpenStreetMap’s public map for places near you.
  • What actually leaves your phone: the latitude and longitude, to Google to look up your town, and to OpenStreetMap inside the map search. The app adds no name and no account of its own to either; Google and OpenStreetMap handle what they receive under their own privacy policies.
  • You’re always asked first. The location notice appears after you tap My Location and before Android’s permission prompt. Its exact words:
The location notice, word for word

“To find churches and services near you, this app sends your location to Google, to look up your town, and to OpenStreetMap’s Overpass servers, to search for places nearby.”

If you’d rather not use GPS at all, use Zip Code or Select State instead — both find places without any location access. A ZIP code is looked up with Google the same way, to find where it is.

Part 11

Where your information lives

The short version: your information stays on your phone. The app is built so that journals, profile, recovery contacts, and emergency info are stored locally and are deliberately excluded from cloud backup — they don’t get swept into Google’s automatic backup. Nothing is uploaded to a server the app runs, because there isn’t one.

What the app holds, and when anything leaves

HoldsYour journal, personal profile, prayers, bookmarks, recovery contacts, saved resources, emergency info.
WhereLocal databases on your phone.
Leaves the device?Only for features you use: your location or ZIP code, to Google and OpenStreetMap, for a nearby search (Part 10); usage reports from Google ML Kit, the text reader behind Scan a Page; and, only when you tap Check for Updates, a request to GitHub for newer prayers and lists.

Updates to the prayers and lists

The prayers, Personal Assistance, Churches, Recovery Road, A Kentucky Path and the Step workbook’s questions can get corrections and additions without a new version of the app. Open Help and tap Check for Updates, or tap Check on the card that appears on the home screen about every 90 days.

  • The app only looks when you tap. Nothing is checked at start-up, on a timer, or in the background, and there are no notifications.
  • You see it before it downloads. Check lists what is new and how big it is; nothing downloads until you tap Download updates.
  • Who can see it: the updates are kept on GitHub. Like any website, GitHub can see your phone’s internet address when you check or download. Nothing you have written is sent.
  • Every update is signed and checked on your phone before it is used. One that cannot be confirmed as genuine is not downloaded.
  • Your own things stay as they are. Your journal, notes, saved prayers and the prayers you wrote are never changed by an update. If an update rewords a prayer you have saved, the wording you saved is copied into your own prayers first, labelled with the month (“Saved wording, March 2027”).
  • A prayer that changed shows its earlier wording. Its page gains an Earlier wording tab, with Keep this wording to copy it into your own prayers.
  • The app keeps a fallback. The wording the app came with is never deleted, and the phone keeps the last two updates. If an update ever fails to load, the app quietly uses the one before it.
